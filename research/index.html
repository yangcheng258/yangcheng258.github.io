Prioritizing Shelter, Sacrificing Nutrition: Housing Stress, Stigma, and Rural Health

Erin Gaede, Yang Cheng & Tessa Conroy
Under review at Rural Sociology.
Slides DOI Repo Cite
›

This study employs a mixed-method design to examine the relationship between housing financial stress and food insecurity and its implications for rural health. We combine qualitative interviews with U.S. county-level data to identify correlations between housing financial stress and nutritional outcomes, stratified by rural-urban status, tenure, and income. Our findings demonstrate how the combined burden of housing financial stress and food insecurity shapes chronic health outcomes for low-income rural communities and the role that stigma plays in shaping how rural residents navigate these dual insecurities.

Move, Stay, and Commit: Place-based Quality of Life in the U.S.

Yang Cheng, Tessa Conroy & Steven C. Deller
Social Indicators Research.
Slides DOI Repo Cite
›

Attracting and retaining people and jobs through a high quality of life is an increasingly common community development strategy. Identifying places with high quality of life, and the factors that drive it, remain challenging. This study uses a revealed-preference framework, based on people’s observed choices and behaviors, to develop an indicator of place-based quality of life. We conceptualize quality of life as a latent variable reflected in eight behaviors capturing decisions to move, stay, and invest. Using spatial Bayesian factor analysis, we construct a revealed-preference quality-of-life index that accounts for spatial spillovers and rank 3,100 U.S. counties in 2019. High-quality-of-life counties are indicated by dynamic migration patterns, strong residential retention, shorter commutes, lower homeownership rates, and higher rates of entrepreneurship and family formation. Four indicators, retention, homeownership, in-migration, and commuting, account for 87 percent of the index weight. The index reveals strong spatial spillovers, highlighting the importance of regional context. While urban counties rank higher on average, many rural counties also exhibit high quality of life.

Multidimensional Measures of Quality of Life: A Comparison of Methods Using U.S. County-level Data

Yang Cheng, Tessa Conroy & Steven C. Deller
Papers in Regional Science.
Slides DOI Repo Cite
›

There are numerous measures of place-based quality of life, accompanied by ongoing debate over which factors should be included. Past studies have predominately relied on aspatial, subjectively weighted combinations of different place-based characteristics. Little attention has been given to the rigor and appropriateness of the methodologies used to develop these measures. In this study we conduct a sensitivity analysis of methodological choices in the construction of quality of life indices using U.S. county-level data. We evaluate six different methods for developing a composite index, comparing Geographically Weighted Principal Component Analysis (GWPCA), Spatial Principal Component Analysis (SPCA), and Spatial Bayesian Factor Analysis (SBFA) with their aspatial counterparts. Our evaluation is twofold: conceptually, by examining underlying assumptions of each method, and practically, by assessing the consistency of results. We compare U.S. county-level quality of life ranking across methods and levels of rurality. While quality of life measures are generally highly correlated across methods, we find that the choice of spatial method does affect the rankings of individual counties. Notably, the most urban and most rural counties exhibit the greatest sensitivity to methodological choice. These findings underscore the importance of sensitivity analysis, particularly in urban and rural contexts, to better understand and account for the complex and spatially varying factors shaping quality of life.

WIndicator: The impact of housing financial stress and food insecurity on rural health

Yang Cheng, Erin Gaede & Tessa Conroy
UW–Madison Division of Extension.
Slides DOI Repo Cite
›

In this WIndicator, we explore how poverty shapes rural health outcomes by evaluating upstream stressors such as housing financial stress and food insecurity. We first establish the association between rural health, poverty, food insecurity and housing at the national level. We then focus on Wisconsin by turning to insights gleaned from interviews to explore why these patterns emerge. We find that poverty, which is more prevalent in rural places, is associated with food insecurity and housing stress. These correlations suggest a compounding effect of housing stress and food insecurity in vulnerable households that may contribute to chronic disease. Our interviews further reveal that rural stigma and remoteness shape how housing stress and food insecurity interact: fewer food options and social pressures to hide financial struggles result in households absorbing housing stresses by cutting back on nutritious food, directly undermining health.

A Green Skill-based Recommendation System

Yang Cheng & Susan Chen
Under review at PLOS ONE.
Slides DOI Repo Cite
›

Shifts in the energy mix can lead to long-term structural changes in the labor market, including the displacement of labor. Technological investments in green energy and the development of green industries have led to an evolution of new green jobs and a demand for a new set of workplace skills. Displaced workers may require new skills to transition into greener jobs, while workers new to the labor market may need to acquire the skills to find employment in a greener economy. For both types of workers, understanding the skill needed for these new jobs, as well as their transferability and overlap with existing jobs, is important. In this paper, we develop a green skill-based recommendation system. We first identify a core set of green skills by regressing a job’s “greenness” score on the full set of skills using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression. Then we measure how dissimilar those jobs are with a Euclidean distance across green skills. The system identifies transition options between jobs within the same level of career preparation by visually displaying similar jobs that utilize their existing skills. By decomposing the dissimilarity through a comparison of green skills, the system clearly outlines the re-skilling requirements for successful transitions. This system equips job seekers, career switchers, and policymakers with information about potential green career options and the re-skilling requirement based on their current skill endowments.

Occupational Green Potential: A Continuous Task-based Measure of Green Work

Susan Chen, Yang Cheng & Andrew Katz
Working paper.
Slides DOI Repo Cite
›

Amidst the challenges of climate change and energy insecurity, many economies worldwide are undergoing a transformation towards enhanced environmental sustainability, reduced emissions, and improved energy security. However, there is no consensus on how to determine the greenness of jobs from an occupational perspective. We propose a task-based approach that uses natural language processing to measure the greenness of jobs. We embed task descriptions into a vector space, cluster all tasks in the economy, and use green task labels from O*NET to score each cluster. We then aggregate task-level scores into an Occupational Green Potential index for all 923 occupations. The Occupational Green Potential index is continuous and captures the degree to which a job’s tasks and underlying skills align with those of green occupations. We validate the index against existing measures and find high correlations. Our approach also detects green potential in occupations not typically classified as green, whose tasks involve environmental controls and land remediation. By grounding the measurement of green jobs in the skills and tasks workers actually perform, our approach offers a new way to assess the green potential of jobs that can be extended to different economies and updated over time as the content of work evolves.

Green Technology and Local Employment in China: A Task-Based Analysis of Green and Brown Jobs

Yang Cheng & Lianyi Hu
Under review at Research Policy.
Slides DOI Repo Cite
›

This paper examines the impact of green technology innovation on employment in China between 1990 and 2015. Green technologies create new employment opportunities while displacing jobs in polluting industries within local labor markets. To assess their employment effects within a task-based framework, we classify green and brown jobs and trace their creation and destruction through job titles and descriptions. We then use Chinese Population Census data to construct measures of overall, green, and brown employment at the prefecture level, and link them to patent-based indicators of green innovation. Our findings show that green technology has no significant effect on aggregate employment because its positive impact on green employment is offset by a reduction in brown employment across prefectures. Further analysis indicates that demand effects, defined as changes in existing jobs, account for most of the impact, while the creation of new green jobs also contributes, though to a smaller extent. The effects are particularly pronounced in commercial occupations and among less educated workers. These results highlight the distributional consequences of green technological change and the need for re-skilling policies to support a green transition.
